# Alpha terrain shader color numerics

Status: FINAL / STABLE / HELD after the complete readback recorded by the author. This is an independent, code-free account of the ordinary terrain programs and their directly traced CPU inputs. It does not establish the transfer from shader output to a displayed pixel.

## Vertex inputs and uniform map

The initializer's declaration has stream zero position at offset zero with numeric type `5`, and color at offset four with numeric type `4`. These are respectively four unsigned bytes and packed D3DCOLOR. Position supplies three unnormalized coordinate bytes and a fourth face-selector byte. Packed color supplies normalized R, G, B, A; in the eight-byte little-endian record the color bytes at offsets four through seven are B, G, R, A. Thus color normalization is a declaration conversion, before vertex lighting. There is no shader texture lookup that supplies ordinary terrain color. ## Vertex position, face normal, and interpolated auxiliaries

The vertex program preserves the three coordinate-byte values and gives their position a homogeneous fourth component of one. It calculates clip Z by a four-component dot against vertex register 22, clip W by a dot against register 23, and the reciprocal of clip W. It then calculates clip X by a dot against register 20 and multiplies X by that reciprocal for the first fog auxiliary. Clip Y follows by a dot against register 21; a multiply-add of clip Y, that reciprocal, and register 31's first component supplies the second auxiliary. The stored clip position receives the unmodified clip X, Y, Z, W results. The reciprocal and multiply-add are explicit operations; a newly calculated Euclidean view angle is not substituted. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, vertex program at RVA `0x2FD928`.]

Three four-component dots against registers 27–29 produce world X, Y, Z. Camera position minus that world position gives the distance vector. A three-component self-dot gives full squared distance. One masked vector multiplication separately obtains distance X squared and distance Y squared; an addition takes Y squared plus X squared for horizontal squared distance. The program squares fog scale once and multiplies full squared distance by that square for the third fog auxiliary and horizontal squared distance by the same square for the fourth. These quantities are calculated per vertex and then transported to the pixel stage; the pixel shader does not recalculate distance from an interpolated world position. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, vertex program.]

The face byte selects the following exact axis normal before transformation:

| Face byte | Local normal |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Positive X |
| 1 | Negative X |
| 2 | Positive Y |
| 3 | Negative Y |
| 4 | Positive Z |
| Every other unsigned-byte value | Negative Z |

The selection uses equality masks and interpolations, not a bounded array access. Three three-component dots against the world columns transform this normal. One native normalize operation then supplies the world normal for all lighting. No inverse-transpose computation occurs in this shader. Separately, the local normal is transformed by three dots against the world/view columns, normalized, and doubled. Its first two components are available to the pixel program's optional oscillation. The vertex program also emits the directional vector and camera-distance vector on additional texture-coordinate semantics, but the ordinary pixel program does not declare or consume those extra outputs. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, vertex and ordinary pixel programs.]

## Vertex lighting and channel order

All RGB operations in this section are componentwise unless a dot is named. The first directional factor is the dot of the normalized world normal with the uploaded directional vector, followed by a maximum with zero. A multiply-add combines that factor times front RGB with ambient RGB. A second dot uses the negated directional vector and the same normal, again followed by a maximum with zero. A second multiply-add adds that back factor times back RGB to the first result. The program separately multiplies material alpha by vertex alpha. This alpha product scales the ambient/front/back result when point light zero is combined with it. Point lights are not themselves scaled by this alpha product. The shader therefore gives the stored vertex alpha a lighting role even though the final pixel alpha comes from a uniform. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, vertex program.]

Each point light uses world position minus that light's position. A self-dot gives squared distance. A reciprocal-square-root operation on that dot, followed by vector multiplication, gives the direction used in the normal dot. The normal dot is multiplied by negative four, then raised to at least binary32 `0.3` and lowered to at most one. The direction is vertex-minus-light: the negative-four multiplication is part of the observed orientation and cannot be omitted by renaming the vector. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, vertex program.]

The radius is squared, its reciprocal is taken, and squared distance is multiplied by that reciprocal. The resulting ratio is lowered to at most one. The shader subtracts this capped ratio from one, squares the remainder, and multiplies the angular factor by that square. It then multiplies the light RGB by the resulting scalar. An enabled mask is one precisely when the light's radius is strictly greater than zero, otherwise zero. There is no preliminary branch that skips the distance or reciprocal arithmetic for disabled lights. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, vertex program.]

Light zero's RGB is multiplied by its enabled mask as a separate operation. A multiply-add then combines the directional/ambient RGB times the alpha product with this masked light-zero RGB. Lights one through nine are accumulated in increasing numeric order, each by a multiply-add of its enabled mask times its RGB contribution plus the preceding accumulator. The second light interleaves its radius-ratio cap and angular cap in one masked vector minimum, but preserves the same two scalar caps. No summation tree, reordering by magnitude, or final average appears. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, vertex program.]

After light nine, the shader applies a componentwise maximum with zero and then a componentwise minimum with binary32 `1.2`. It computes one minus this clamped lighting RGB. In separate operations it multiplies the lighting RGB by vertex RGB, then multiplies that product by material RGB. A multiply-add adds darkness RGB times the saved lighting complement to this product. It next computes one minus this current color and uses a multiply-add of white amount times that complement plus the current color. That is the vertex color supplied to the pixel stage. There is no shader saturation after material RGB, darkness, or the white blend. The vertex color's alpha is copied directly from vertex alpha. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, vertex program.]

This ordering permits an RGB channel above one. The lighting cap is `1.2`, and the program does not replace it with a final color cap of one. Shader Model 3 color input semantics do not impose the older color-register clamp; the documented pixel input registers carry floating-point values without a color-specific clamp. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, vertex program. API boundary: [ps_3_0 registers](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/direct3dhlsl/dx9-graphics-reference-asm-ps-registers-ps-3-0).]

## Ordinary pixel color and fog order

The pixel program consumes interpolated vertex RGB, all four fog auxiliaries, and the first two components of the doubled view normal. It contains no texture sampler declaration or texture sample. Its first sky blend uses the second fog auxiliary, namely the interpolated clip-Y/clip-W value plus the CPU translation already added at the vertex stage. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, ordinary pixel program at RVA `0x2FEB50`.]

Three places in this pixel program use the same smooth cubic sequence. For an already bounded scalar, it first squares the scalar, multiplies that square by three, and then performs a two-component dot-and-add: both dot terms multiply the square by the negated original scalar, and the addend is the previously calculated three-times-square. The real-number result is three times the square minus twice the cube. That algebraic identity describes the shape; the actual boundaries are square, multiply-by-three, and one native two-component dot-and-add, in that order. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, ordinary pixel program. API operation meaning: [dp2add](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/direct3dhlsl/dp2add---ps).]

First, the shader multiplies the vertical auxiliary by binary32 `0.6`, clamps that product to zero through one with a separate saturating move, and runs the cubic sequence. It subtracts first-sky RGB from second-sky RGB, then uses a multiply-add of the cubic times that difference plus first-sky RGB. This is the upper sky gradient. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, ordinary pixel program.]

Second, a saturating multiply-add takes twice the vertical auxiliary plus one, and the cubic sequence acts on that result. In parallel, the shader multiplies fog RGB by binary32 `0.8`, then uses a multiply-add of first-sky RGB times binary32 `0.2` plus that fog product. A native linear interpolation combines the upper sky gradient as its first color and this fog/first-sky mixture as its second color, using the second cubic as the weight of the first color. This produces the sky RGB used at long horizontal distance. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, ordinary pixel program.]

Third, the horizontal squared-distance auxiliary has binary32 `0.6` subtracted from it. Multiplication by exactly `2.5` is saturated to zero through one; the cubic sequence acts on it; its result is squared once more. This last square is the far-distance sky weight. It is not the original saturated distance weight, and it is not a fourth power of that original weight. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, ordinary pixel program.]

The full squared-distance auxiliary independently enters a saturating multiply-add: multiply by exactly `1.5` and add binary32 `-0.025`. The shader subtracts that saturated result from one. A multiply-add then multiplies this complement by its own negation and adds one. Finally it multiplies by exactly `0.75`. This is the near-distance fog weight. A native linear interpolation uses that weight for fog RGB and its complement for interpolated vertex RGB. A second native linear interpolation uses the far-distance sky weight for the previously calculated sky RGB and its complement for the near-fog result. Thus the near fog blend precedes the long-distance sky blend. None of the final color channels receives an explicit saturation in these blends. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, ordinary pixel program.]

The remaining arithmetic computes an optional oscillating multiplier. A two-component dot-and-add combines ten times the second fog auxiliary, five times the first fog auxiliary (clip X divided by clip W at the vertex), and the second component of the doubled view normal. A separate addition adds its first component. A multiply-add scales that phase by binary32 `0.15915493738651276` (`0x3E22F983`) and adds `0.5`; a fractional-part operation follows. A multiply-add then multiplies by binary32 `6.2831854820251465` (`0x40C90FDB`) and adds binary32 `-3.1415927410125732` (`0xC0490FDB`). The cosine output of the native sine/cosine operation is multiplied by binary32 `0.3` and added to one by a multiply-add. The resulting scalar multiplies all three color channels from the preceding fog/sky blend. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, ordinary pixel program. API channel meaning: [sincos](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/direct3dhlsl/sincos---ps).]

A native comparison against the negated pixel-register-four scalar selects the unmodulated fog/sky RGB when that scalar is nonpositive, and the oscillated RGB when it is positive, for finite scalar values. Its magnitude is not a specular exponent. All preceding oscillation arithmetic remains present regardless of this selection. Output alpha is copied from pixel register three's first component; vertex alpha and sky/fog alpha are not inputs to this output alpha. [Original: `binary/Cube.exe`, ordinary pixel program.]

## Directly traced CPU inputs on the terrain path

The rendering object contains an embedded world beginning at `+0x2E4`. The renderer's signed 32-bit time at object `+0x800440` is therefore the same address as world `+0x80015C`, which the environment producer reads. This account uses those addresses as the time identity; it does not claim how the clock advances or wraps. [Original: `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x0AD478`, `0x0AD6DD–0x0AD7A1`, `0x19D65B–0x19D67F`.]

The material alpha is calculated near the beginning of the renderer. The signed time converts to binary32, is multiplied by two, divided by binary32 `86400000`, and has one subtracted, with a separate binary32 result at each arithmetic instruction. That result is promoted to binary64 and passed to the imported precise power routine with binary64 exponent four. The returned double narrows to binary32. One minus that narrowed value is calculated in binary32, promoted to binary64, and passed to the same routine with binary64 exponent five. The second result narrows to binary32 and becomes material alpha. No clamp or time modulo is performed in this sequence. [Original: `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x0AC3E1–0x0AC44A`; `decompiled/client/functions/0068d8d4.c`; `binary/Cube.exe`, data RVAs `0x302AC4`, `0x302B50`, `0x302B58`, `0x345E10`.]

Material RGB is initially `(1, 1, 1)`. The renderer queries the cell at its position and tests whether the low five bits of the cell's fourth byte equal two. Under that numeric condition material RGB becomes `(0.4, 0.5, 1)`; material alpha remains the time curve just described. The terrain-chunk loop uploads that material record at RVA `0x0B1BF5`. The environmental meaning and admission rules of this cell query are outside this contract; the numeric condition and resulting tint are established. [Original: `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x0AD684–0x0AD72F`, `0x0B1BE7–0x0B1BF5`; `decompiled/client/functions/00448280.c`.]

The directional vector uses the same signed clock. The renderer converts time to binary32, multiplies by two in binary32, promotes that value to binary64, multiplies by the binary64 value of pi `3.141592653589793`, and divides by binary64 `86400000`. That angle narrows to binary32 before being promoted separately for the imported precise cosine and sine routines. Each returned double narrows to binary32. The input to the light setter is `(0, negative sine, negative cosine)`. The angle path is distinct from the material and environment power curves. [Original: `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x0AD7A6–0x0AD822`; `decompiled/client/functions/0068d7a4.c`, `0068d9c2.c`; `binary/Cube.exe`, data RVAs `0x2FCE08`, `0x302B60`.]

RVA `0x048170` uploads front RGB, back RGB, and ambient RGB before normalizing its direction argument. For that normalization it squares X and Y separately in binary32, adds those products, squares Z, and adds it to the preceding sum. The squared length promotes to binary64 for the imported precise square root. The root narrows to binary32, exactly one is divided by it in binary32, and each original component is multiplied by that binary32 reciprocal. A fourth component of zero is packed with the three products. There is no zero-length test in this CPU setter. The ordinary terrain path calls it at RVA `0x0B1A9C` with the renderer's saved environment front and back colors, rotating direction, and saved ambient color. [Original: `decompiled/client/functions/00448170.c`; `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x048170–0x048274`, `0x0B1A76–0x0B1A9C`.]

RVA `0x048100` updates fog scale only when its supplied float is strictly greater than zero. It uploads binary32 one divided by that float, with three zero components. On the terrain path the argument is rendering-object `+0x1D4`. A zero, negative, or unordered argument takes the no-upload path and retains device constant state; this site does not establish a replacement zero. [Original: `decompiled/client/functions/00448100.c`; `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x048110–0x048169`, `0x0B1AA1–0x0B1AB5`.]

RVA `0x048090` subtracts exactly eighty from its argument in binary32 and divides that result by exactly negative thirty in binary32. It uploads the result and three zero components as fog-gradient translation. The renderer supplies ninety when byte `+0x8006E4` is nonzero, otherwise float `+0x1A4`. This assignment occurs earlier in the renderer at RVA `0x0ADA3D`; the ordinary terrain binding does not itself repeat it. [Original: `decompiled/client/functions/00448090.c`; `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x0ADA17–0x0ADA3D`; `binary/Cube.exe`, data RVAs `0x2FCE50`, `0x345E9C`.]

The camera-position assignment constructs three signed 64-bit coordinate sums from the rendering object's three components beginning at `+0x140`, plus the two 64-bit components at `+0x1D8` and `+0x1E0` and a zero third addend. Each sum converts through x87 and is stored as binary32, then multiplied in binary32 by exactly `1/65536`. RVA `0x048010` packs those results with a zero fourth component and uploads vertex register 36. Integer addition carries within each coordinate; this is not a prior floating-point addition of separately scaled positions. The detailed camera-origin producer is outside scope. [Original: `decompiled/client/functions/0042c800.c`, `00448010.c`; `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x0AD840–0x0AD92C`; `binary/Cube.exe`, data RVA `0x2FCD94`.]

The ordinary terrain path directly uploads darkness `(0, 0, 0, 1)` at RVA `0x0B1AEF`, point positions/colors from each chunk at RVA `0x0B1C0B`, and output alpha exactly one at RVA `0x0B1C1E`. Earlier explicit assignments set white amount to zero at RVA `0x0AD73D` and the pixel oscillation selector to zero at RVA `0x0AD9A9`. The renderer uploads its saved first sky, second sky, and fog vectors at RVAs `0x0AD9CC` and `0x0AED8C`. Retention of the earlier assignments through every intervening nested rendering call is not established here. The setter table and shader math remain applicable to whichever values are effective. In particular, the unmodulated pixel result is the consequence when the established zero selector remains effective, not a claim that an untraced path cannot change it. [Original: `decompiled/client/functions/00448070.c`, `00447fb0.c`, `00448fe0.c`, `00449040.c`, `00449090.c`; `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, cited renderer RVAs.]

The terrain draw calls matrix setter RVA `0x0482A0` with three sixteen-float matrices: world, view, and projection. It uploads three transposed world columns to registers 27–29, computes world times view with binary32 scalar products/additions, and uploads three transposed columns of that result to registers 24–26. Separately it computes view times projection, then world times that saved result, and uploads all four transposed columns to registers 20–23. It does not obtain the latter by multiplying the already calculated world/view result by projection. Each four-term scalar sum uses separate binary32 multiplications and successive additions, rather than a CPU fused multiply-add. Matrix construction, camera orientation, and projection settings are bounded upstream inputs here. [Original: `decompiled/client/functions/004482a0.c`; `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVA `0x0482A0–0x048EFE`, terrain argument setup `0x0B1D22–0x0B1D40`.]

## CPU environment palette, with spatial generators bounded

RVA `0x19D640` receives the embedded world, the renderer's three-component signed 64-bit position, and six output color records. The outputs, in argument order after position, are first sky, second sky, fog, front light, back light, and ambient. The renderer preserves these records and passes them to the setters above. The following account closes their RGB palette arithmetic downstream of four spatial scalar results; it does not recover those generators' internal noise, seed, cache, or biome rules. [Original: `decompiled/client/functions/0059d640.c`; `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x0AD768–0x0AD7A1`, `0x19D640–0x19ED26`.]

For each spatial query the two horizontal signed 64-bit position components are divided by signed `65536` using the original integer helper. The low 32-bit results are supplied as the two coordinates. Scalar A is returned by RVA `0x1C4DD0`, scalar B by RVA `0x1C4800`, and scalar Q by RVA `0x1EF040`. A fourth scalar C is requested from RVA `0x1F0720` only on the branch specified below. Each returned x87 value stores to binary32 before the palette arithmetic consumes it. These are neutral scalar names, not inferred temperature, moisture, weather, or biome classifications. No range for A, B, C, or Q is proved by this contract. [Original: `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x19D811–0x19D932`, `0x19DA1E–0x19DA56`, `0x19DD6A–0x19DDA2`; `decompiled/client/functions/0059d640.c`.]

The environment time weight is calculated separately from material alpha. Signed time converts to binary32, is multiplied by two, divided by binary32 `86400000`, and has one subtracted in binary32. After promotion to binary64 its sign bit is cleared for absolute value; it narrows to binary32 and promotes again before a precise power call with binary64 exponent three. That result narrows to binary32. One minus that narrowed result is calculated in binary32, promoted, and passed to a precise power call with binary64 exponent four. The final result narrows to binary32. Call this final environment time weight T. There is no clamp or modulo in this producer. [Original: `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x19D65B–0x19D708`; `binary/Cube.exe`, data RVAs `0x2FCB98`, `0x302AC4`, `0x302B50`, `0x345F50`.]

Front and back light use these base RGB palette points:

| T position | Front RGB | Back RGB |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zero | `(0.01, 0.02, 0.01)` | `(0, 0.02, 0.03)` |
| One half | `(1, 0.9, 0.6)` | `(0.6, 0.5, 1)` |
| One | `(1.1, 1.1, 0.9)` | `(0.5, 0.6, 0.8)` |

When Q is strictly positive, these six RGB points first change toward the following six targets. The CPU calculates one minus Q in binary32, calculates the Q-times-target and complement-times-base products separately in binary32, then adds those products. It does not saturate Q or the resulting RGB.

| T position | Front target RGB | Back target RGB |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zero | `(1, 0.8, 0.1)` | `(0.7, 0.3, 0.3)` |
| One half | `(0.8, 0.8, 0)` | `(0.8, 0.4, 0.4)` |
| One | `(1, 0.8, 0.1)` | `(1, 0.5, 0.5)` |

For T at most one half, the producer multiplies T by two, subtracts that result from one, and adds complement-times-zero-point to doubled-T-times-half-point, using separate binary32 products/addition in each RGB channel. For T above one half, it subtracts one half from T and multiplies by two; the output adds this weight times the one-point to its complement times the half-point. There is no upper clamp on T in these two final light branches. The shader uses only the RGB components of front and back colors; their output alpha calculation is outside this RGB contract. [Original: `decompiled/client/functions/0059d640.c`; `decompiled/client/assembly.txt`, RVAs `0x19E7B5–0x19ED26`.]

## Precision, interpolation, and explicit limits

CPU widths above come from original scalar SSE instructions, conversions, x87 stores, and imported routine identities. CPU scalar products and additions described as binary32 have separate native boundaries. Imported precise power, sine, cosine, and square-root calls expose binary64 arguments/results at these sites, but their internal approximation and floating-point-environment behavior are not recovered here. Signed clock and coordinate conversions are not an assertion that every possible malformed value produces a meaningful rendered scene.

The shader bytecode establishes the sequence and grouping of scalar/vector operations, literal binary32 constants, destination masks, saturation locations, dot products, multiply-adds, reciprocal, reciprocal-square-root, normalize, interpolation, and cosine. Neither program contains partial-precision modifiers. Pixel texture-coordinate declarations likewise do not request partial precision. Official Direct3D documentation specifies full precision without those modifiers, saturation to zero through one at the marked operations, and automatic centroid treatment for a pixel input declared as color. This is not a proof of IEEE binary32 rounding after every GPU operation, of a particular fused-versus-separated physical implementation of a native multiply-add or dot, or of bit-identical results across devices.
